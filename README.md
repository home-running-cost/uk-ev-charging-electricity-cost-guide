# How Much Does It Cost to Charge an EV at Home in the UK? A Guide to Electricity Tariffs

If you own an electric vehicle or you're thinking about buying one, you've probably wondered how much it will add to your electricity bill.

Charging at home is convenient, but the cost can vary a lot depending on your electricity tariff, battery size, and when you plug in.

Some households pay a single electricity rate throughout the day, while others use tariffs that offer cheaper overnight electricity.

In this guide, we'll explain how EV charging costs are calculated, how Economy 7 tariffs work, and how to decide whether an overnight tariff could save you money.

## 1. How Are EV Charging Costs Calculated?

The basic calculation is fairly straightforward.

You need to know three things:

- **Battery capacity:** How much energy your EV battery can store, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh).
- **Charging percentage:** How much charge you're adding to the battery.
- **Electricity rate:** How much your supplier charges for each kWh.

One other thing to consider: charging losses.

Not every unit of electricity drawn from your home reaches the battery. Some energy is lost during the charging process, so your electricity meter will usually record slightly more energy than the battery receives.

### A Simple Example

Let's say your electric car has a 60 kWh battery.

You arrive home with 20% charge remaining and want to charge it to 80%.

First, calculate how much energy the battery needs:

60 × (80% − 20%) = **36 kWh**

Now let's assume a charging loss of 10%.

36 ÷ 0.90 = **40 kWh**

That means you'll draw approximately 40 kWh from your electricity supply to complete the charging session.

Now we can work out the cost.

If your electricity rate is 30p per kWh:

40 × £0.30 = **£12.00**

But if you're on a tariff offering an overnight rate of 10p per kWh:

40 × £0.10 = **£4.00**

That's an £8 difference for the same charging session.

These rates are just examples. Your actual cost will depend on your tariff and charging conditions.

## 3. What Is an Economy 7 Electricity Tariff?

You may have come across the term Economy 7 while comparing electricity plans.

It's a type of electricity tariff that charges different rates depending on the time of day.

You typically get:

- **A daytime rate**, which is generally higher.
- **A nighttime rate**, which is lower for a seven-hour off-peak period.

The exact off-peak hours depend on your supplier, meter and location.

Economy 7 was traditionally popular with households using electric storage heaters. However, it can also be worth considering if you have an EV that you regularly charge overnight.

There is a catch, though.

A cheaper nighttime rate doesn't automatically mean a cheaper electricity bill.

If most of your household's electricity is used during the day, the higher daytime rate could cancel out any savings you make overnight.

That's why it's important to compare your total electricity usage rather than looking at the night rate alone.

## 4. Economy 7 vs a Standard Electricity Tariff

Let's look at a practical example.

Imagine a household uses 3,400 kWh of electricity each year.

For this example, we'll compare the following hypothetical tariffs:

| Electricity tariff | Unit rate |
|---|---|
| Standard single-rate tariff | 26p/kWh |
| Economy 7 daytime rate | 30p/kWh |
| Economy 7 nighttime rate | 16p/kWh |

We'll assume the household uses 20% of its electricity overnight.

### Economy 7 Calculation

Nighttime electricity consumption:

3,400 × 20% = **680 kWh**

Daytime electricity consumption:

3,400 − 680 = **2,720 kWh**

Daytime electricity cost:

2,720 × £0.30 = £816.00

Nighttime electricity cost:

680 × £0.16 = £108.80

Total annual Economy 7 energy cost:

**£924.80**

### Standard Tariff Calculation

For the same household, using a standard 26p per kWh tariff:

3,400 × £0.26 = **£884.00**

### What Does This Tell Us?

Under these assumptions, the Economy 7 tariff would cost £40.80 more per year in electricity usage charges.

That doesn't mean Economy 7 is always more expensive.

It simply means this household isn't using enough electricity during the cheaper nighttime period to make up for the higher daytime rate.

For these particular rates, the household would need to shift roughly 29% of its electricity consumption to the nighttime period to break even on usage charges.

The example excludes standing charges, which should also be considered when comparing real tariffs.

## 5. How to Check Whether Economy 7 Is Worth It

Before switching electricity tariffs, it's worth looking at your household's actual consumption.

A few questions can help:

- How much electricity do you use during the day?
- Can you charge your EV during off-peak hours?
- Do you use electric heating or other high-energy appliances overnight?
- What are the daytime and nighttime electricity rates?
- Will the new tariff have a different daily standing charge?

The answers can make a significant difference to your annual bill.

## 6. A Few Ways to Keep EV Charging Costs Down

You don't necessarily need the cheapest advertised electricity tariff to reduce your charging costs.

Sometimes, small changes to your charging habits can make a difference.

**Charge during cheaper hours when possible.**

If your tariff offers discounted overnight electricity, scheduling your charging sessions during those hours may save money.

**Compare the whole tariff.**

Don't focus only on the off-peak rate. A higher daytime rate or standing charge can make a tariff less attractive overall.

**Understand charging losses.**

Remember that the electricity drawn from your home supply can be higher than the energy actually stored in your EV battery.

**Review your household's usage.**

An EV may use a significant amount of electricity, but your heating, cooking and other appliances still affect your total bill.

**Check your actual electricity prices.**

Using your supplier's published rates will give you more useful estimates than relying on general averages.

## 7. A Note on the Calculations

These examples use simple energy and cost formulas that can also be implemented in JavaScript.

For EV charging:

**Energy from the wall = Battery energy added ÷ Charging efficiency**

**Charging cost = Energy from the wall × Unit rate**

For Economy 7:

**Annual cost = (Daytime kWh × Day rate) + (Nighttime kWh × Night rate)**

Daily standing charges can be added to calculate a fuller estimated annual electricity bill.

The same formulas can be used to build small calculation tools or to compare electricity tariffs programmatically.

## Final Thoughts

Charging an electric vehicle at home doesn't have to be expensive, but the tariff you choose can make a noticeable difference.

Economy 7 or a dedicated EV tariff may be worth considering if you can use enough electricity during cheaper hours. On the other hand, a standard tariff could still be the better option if most of your electricity is used during the day.

The key is to work with your actual numbers.

Once you know how much electricity you use, when you use it and what your supplier charges, it's much easier to make a sensible comparison.
